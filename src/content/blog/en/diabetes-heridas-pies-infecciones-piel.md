Some wounds announce themselves with pain. Others stay quiet. When you live with diabetes, a blister from new shoes, a split in the heel or a nail edge digging into a toe can go unnoticed, because the foot no longer feels things the way it used to. By the time you spot it, maybe as a stain on your sock, an odd smell or a work boot that suddenly feels tight, an infection may have been spreading underneath for a while.

This article is for people with diabetes and for the relatives who help them keep an eye on their feet: what happens to the skin, how to look at your feet at home, and why a small wound is worth showing early to the medical team at Clínica Hispana Cruz 2. If you're after the numbers to track (A1C, blood pressure, cholesterol), they're in our [guide to living with diabetes in north Houston](/blog/control-diabetes-houston-guia-pacientes). Here we stick to skin and feet.

## Three reasons feet get hurt without you knowing

1. **Nerves that feel less.** Over time, high blood sugar can damage the nerves in the feet, a problem called neuropathy. Some people notice tingling or burning; others feel almost nothing. With no pain to warn you, a pebble in your shoe or a seam that rubs can wear the skin open over a whole work shift.
2. **Slower circulation.** When less blood reaches the feet, the skin dries out, cracks easily and any wound takes longer to close.
3. **An immune system working overtime.** High glucose makes it easier for bacteria and fungus to settle into the skin. A scrape that would heal without fuss on someone else can turn into a deeper infection on a diabetic foot.

That is why MedlinePlus, in its page on [taking care of your feet with diabetes](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/patientinstructions/000081.htm), recommends checking them every day, even when nothing hurts.

## Small injuries you shouldn't put off

| What you see | Why it matters with diabetes |
|---|---|
| Blister from new shoes or work boots | Once it pops, you have an open wound right where the rubbing happens all day |
| Crack in the heel | Dry skin splits, and bacteria get in through the gap |
| White, soggy, itchy skin between the toes | It may be fungus (athlete's foot), which also cracks the skin |
| Nail digging into the side of the toe | The toe turns red, swells and may drain pus |
| Callus that darkens or stains your sock | A wound may be hiding underneath |
| Red, warm, painful lump | It may be an abscess that needs draining |
| Cut or scrape that never quite closes | A slow wound can get infected without causing a fever |

## The nightly foot check, step by step

Pick a set moment, like when you pull off your boots or right before bed:

- Sit somewhere well lit and look at the sole, the heel, both sides and the space between each toe.
- If you can't see the bottom of your foot, use a hand mirror or ask someone at home to look.
- Watch for cuts, blisters, redness, swelling, color changes or spots that feel warmer than the rest of the foot.
- Before putting shoes on, run your hand around the inside in case there's a stone, a tack or a raised seam.
- Wash your feet in lukewarm water, testing it with your elbow rather than your foot, and dry them well, especially between the toes.
- Rub lotion into dry heels, but not between the toes, where moisture encourages fungus.

If something looks off, snap a photo with your phone. Comparing those pictures at the visit makes it easier to tell whether the spot is growing, changing color or getting better.

## Home habits that tend to make things worse

Some remedies get handed down from grandparents to grandkids, and with diabetes it's better to let them go:

- **Trimming calluses or an ingrown nail with a razor blade or scissors.** It is one of the most common ways to open a wound that later gets infected. Our [ingrown toenail](/servicios/unas-encarnadas) page puts it plainly: with diabetes, don't try to dig the nail out yourself.
- **Soaking your feet in very hot water or using a heating pad.** If your foot can't feel properly, you can burn it without noticing.
- **Covering the wound with pastes, powders, leaves or creams nobody recommended.** They make cleaning harder and hide what the wound really looks like.
- **Popping a blister or a lump with a needle.** That can push the infection deeper.
- **Going barefoot**, even indoors or out in the yard.
- **Putting the shoe that hurt you right back on.** While the skin heals, roomy or open footwear keeps the rubbing from reopening it.

## Is the wound healing or getting infected?

A wound that is healing gets smaller, with pink edges and no smell. One that is going the wrong way usually drops a few hints:

- The skin around it turns red and warm, and that patch keeps getting bigger.
- Pus or cloudy fluid seeps out and stains your sock.
- It smells bad.
- It hurts more, or the foot swells, even though it didn't bother you before.
- The edges look dark, white or purple.
- Your blood sugar climbs for no clear reason, even though you're eating the same as always.

People often overlook that last one: an infection can push glucose out of control before any fever shows up. If you notice any of these signs, don't hold off until your next routine checkup.

## When to call 911 or go to the ER

Go to the emergency room, or call 911 if you can't get there safely, when you notice:

- Redness spreading quickly across the foot or leg, with fever or chills.
- Red streaks running up from the wound toward the ankle or leg.
- A toe or part of the foot turning black, very dark or cold.
- Confusion, heavy drowsiness or feeling very sick.
- Bleeding from the foot that won't stop, even under steady, firm pressure from a clean towel.

## What we do at the clinic with a foot wound

When you come in with a foot injury, the medical team takes off the old dressing, washes and disinfects the area and looks it over carefully: the color of the edges, any drainage, a bad smell or more redness than last time. Then they put on a suitable dressing and tell you when to come back. That follow-up is part of our [wound care](/servicios/curacion-heridas) service, which also covers ulcers and sores on the legs and feet that have stayed open a long time. Because diabetes and poor circulation slow healing, the team keeps that in mind and may check your blood sugar during that visit.

If it's a red, hot lump full of pus, it may be an abscess; in that case the area is numbed with local anesthetic and drained, as described on the [abscess drainage](/servicios/drenaje-abscesos) page. If the nail is the problem, only the ingrown edge is removed. And when the team decides you need medicine for the infection, the prescription is sent electronically to the pharmacy you choose, so you can grab it on your way home.

## After it heals: keeping it from coming back

A closed wound is a good moment to ask why it showed up in the first place. Keeping blood sugar in range helps the skin defend itself, and at a [diabetes care](/servicios/condiciones-cronicas) visit your feet are checked along with your A1C and blood pressure. If it's been a while since your last labs, our [blood tests](/servicios/examenes-sangre) include glucose and hemoglobin A1C. And if you smoke, quitting also improves circulation in your legs.

If you're coming in for a foot wound, wear or bag up the shoes or boots you use every day, along with the socks you had on when you noticed the problem.
