You wake up and even swallowing your own saliva hurts. In the fall, when school is back in session and flu and colds return, that complaint fills our waiting room. The real question behind it is almost always the same: will this go away on its own, or do I need an antibiotic? The answer depends on what's causing it, a virus or group A strep bacteria, and the way to know for sure is a rapid swab test that gives you a result in minutes.

## Why a look in the mirror isn't enough

A red throat looks just as red with a virus as it does with strep. Even white spots on the tonsils can show up with viral infections. That's why guessing from appearance alone often gets it wrong in both directions: some people take leftover antibiotics from another illness for a virus that doesn't need them, and others brush off strep as "just a cold."

The difference matters for two reasons. If it's viral, an antibiotic won't help at all, and it can still give you diarrhea, an allergic reaction or an upset stomach. If it's strep, the antibiotic stops the spread and heads off complications you don't want to gamble on.

## Clues that tip the scale

No single symptom confirms the cause, but certain combinations point one way or the other. This table sums up what each one usually looks like:

| More typical of a virus | More typical of strep |
|---|---|
| Cough and runny nose | No cough, no congestion |
| Hoarse or raspy voice | Sharp pain when swallowing |
| Red or watery eyes | Fever that comes on suddenly |
| Starts gradually, with sneezing | Very red tonsils or white spots |
| Gets better within a few days | Swollen, tender lumps in the neck |

In kids, strep sometimes shows up with a stomachache, nausea or vomiting alongside the sore throat, which throws a lot of parents off because they assume it's a stomach bug.

If what you have is mostly high fever, body aches and a cough, the main suspects may be flu or COVID, and the better choice is the nasal swab we run as part of our [respiratory illness care](/servicios/enfermedades-respiratorias). If symptoms come back every season with itching and sneezing, it may be an allergy rather than an infection.

## Who gets it most?

Strep throat is most common in school-age children, especially between ages 5 and 15, according to the CDC. It's uncommon in kids under 3. Adults catch it too, more often if they live with children or work around a lot of people. It spreads through droplets from coughs and sneezes and through shared cups, utensils or water bottles, so when one child brings it home from class, it's not unusual for someone else in the house to start hurting a few days later.

## What the test looks like, step by step

The rapid strep test happens during your visit and needs no preparation:

1. The medical team asks how long it has hurt and whether you have a fever, cough or runny nose, then checks your throat, tonsils and neck.
2. You tilt your head back, open wide and let your tongue hang out so the tonsils are visible.
3. A swab brushes the tonsils and the back of the throat for a few seconds. It may make you gag, but it doesn't hurt.
4. The sample is processed at the clinic while you wait, and the result is ready in minutes.
5. With the result in hand, the medical team walks you through what comes next.

One practical tip: skip the mouthwash gargle right before you come in, so the sample is as reliable as possible.

## If it's positive

A positive result confirms that group A strep is behind the pain. The medical team prescribes the right antibiotic and you pick it up at our [pharmacy](/servicios/farmacia) before you leave, with instructions in Spanish or English on how to take it. The key is to **finish every dose**, even if the fever is gone and your throat feels better after two or three days. Stopping halfway makes it more likely the infection comes back.

In the meantime, the sick person should stay home from school or work until the medical team says it's okay, and never while they still have a fever.

## If it's negative

In adults, a negative result usually means the sore throat is viral. No antibiotic will help with that, so the plan focuses on relief: fluids, rest and something for pain and fever if the medical team recommends it. For children and teens when strep still seems likely, the medical team decides whether a throat culture is needed to confirm, because the rapid test can miss some cases.

If the pain gets worse instead of better over the next few days, a high fever shows up, or one side of your throat looks much more swollen than the other, come back for another look.

## What can happen if strep goes untreated

Most strep cases improve, but without an antibiotic the bacteria keeps spreading and can lead to trouble. The complications the CDC describes include an abscess next to the tonsils (a pocket of pus that makes it hard to open your mouth), ear or sinus infections and, less often, rheumatic fever, which can affect the heart and joints. That's why it's worth confirming with a [rapid strep test](/servicios/prueba-strep) rather than waiting to see what happens.

## Home care, whatever the result

- Drink water, warm tea or broth; warm liquids usually go down easier than solid food
- Gargle with warm water and a pinch of salt to calm the irritation
- Try popsicles or cold drinks if heat makes it worse
- Replace your toothbrush once you start feeling better
- Wash cups and utensils separately and don't share bottles at home
- Cover your cough and wash your hands often, especially around babies or older relatives

Over-the-counter lozenges and throat sprays can ease the pain for a while, but they don't get rid of the bacteria. If it's strep, the antibiotic is what clears it.

## When not to wait for the test

Go to an emergency room or call 911 if:

- You can't swallow even your own saliva, or you're drooling
- Breathing is hard or noisy
- You can barely open your mouth, or your neck is very swollen
- There's confusion, bluish lips or strong chest pain

## What people ask us at the front desk

**Can I have strep without a fever?**
Yes. Fever is common, but it doesn't always show up. If swallowing hurts a lot and you have no cough or runny nose, the test is worth doing.

**Does the flu shot protect against strep?**
No. The flu vaccine protects against the influenza virus, not against bacteria. It's still a good idea to get it every season; ask about it through our [vaccines](/servicios/vacunas) service.
