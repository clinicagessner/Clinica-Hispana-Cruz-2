It creeps up quietly. First you start noticing where the restroom is in every store, then you need two stops on a drive you used to make in one go, and one day you realize you're getting up three times a night. Many men live with it for months before mentioning it, out of embarrassment or because they figure "it's just age." Sometimes age does play a part, but urinating all the time is a signal with several possible causes, and almost all of them can be sorted out with simple tests.

In this article, the medical team at Clínica Hispana Cruz 2 helps you read the clues your own body is giving you and explains what gets checked when you come in for this.

## How often is "all the time"?

There's no single number that fits everyone, because it depends on how much you drink, the heat and the kind of work you do. As a general reference, most adults urinate four to eight times during the day and, at most, once at night. It's worth paying attention when:

- You go noticeably more often than a year ago, without changing what you drink.
- The urge hits suddenly and it's hard to make it in time.
- You wake up two or more times every night to urinate.
- Only a little comes out each time, even though your bladder felt full.

## The clues from your own bathroom trips

The way you urinate says a lot. This table doesn't replace a checkup, but it points you toward the cause that might be behind it:

| What you notice | What it often suggests |
|---|---|
| Large amounts each time, strong thirst and dry mouth | High blood sugar |
| Weak stream, slow to start, still feeling the urge after | An enlarged prostate squeezing the passage |
| Burning, cloudy or strong-smelling urine | A urinary tract infection |
| Happens mostly after coffee, sodas or beer | Caffeine or alcohol |
| Sudden urgency with no burning and a normal stream | A bladder that contracts too much |
| Started after a medicine was changed | A side effect of that medicine |

## Cause by cause, in plain words

### The prostate that grows with age

Picture the prostate as a ring hugging the urethra, which carries urine out of the body. It normally grows with age, and this benign enlargement is very common after 50. As it gets bigger, it narrows the passage: the bladder doesn't fully empty and asks to go again a little later. That's why the typical pattern is a weak stream, dribbling at the end and getting up at night. Growth doesn't mean something serious, but it does deserve a check, because a bladder that stays full for a long time can get irritated or infected.

### High blood sugar

When glucose climbs too high, the kidneys try to flush the excess out through the urine and pull a lot of water along with it. The result is passing large amounts of urine, a thirst that won't go away and sometimes losing weight without trying or blurry vision. Many men find out they have diabetes precisely because of this symptom. If you already know you have it and suddenly urinate more, it can mean your control has slipped.

### A urinary tract infection

In men these are less common than in women, so when one shows up it's worth finding out why. Besides the constant urge, they usually bring burning, discomfort in the lower belly or urine that looks different. Sometimes the infection is linked to a prostate that keeps the bladder from emptying well.

### Coffee, sodas, beer and what you drink at night

Caffeine and alcohol make the kidneys produce more urine and also irritate the bladder. If you have three cups of coffee before lunch or several beers in the evening, that alone can explain a good share of your trips to the bathroom.

### A bladder in a hurry

In some men the bladder muscle squeezes too early, even when it isn't full. It feels like a sudden urgency, with no burning and no change in the stream.

## Keep a three-day log before you come in

What helps most at the visit is knowing, with real data, how you urinate. For three days, jot down on your phone:

1. The time of every trip to the bathroom, day and night.
2. Whether the amount was small, medium or large.
3. What you drank and when: water, coffee, soda, beer.
4. Any urgency, dribbling, burning or leaks.

With that log, the medical team can quickly see whether the issue is how much urine you make, how your bladder empties or what you drink.

## What gets checked at Clínica Hispana Cruz 2

The visit starts with questions: how long it's been happening, what your stream is like, how many times you get up at night, which medicines you take and whether diabetes or prostate problems run in your family. Then, based on what you share, the tests are chosen.

- **Urine test.** It's processed at the clinic during your visit and shows signs of infection, blood or sugar in the urine. If there's an infection, treatment is prescribed at that same visit; the [urinary tract infections](/servicios/infecciones-urinarias) page explains how that service works.
- **Blood glucose.** If your urine shows sugar or your symptoms point that way, glucose is measured and, depending on the case, hemoglobin A1C, with [blood tests](/servicios/examenes-sangre) drawn right here.
- **PSA.** This is a protein made by the prostate and measured in the blood. The [men's health](/servicios/salud-hombre) page lists it along with the urine test and vital signs. A raised PSA can come from benign enlargement or an infection, so it's always read alongside your symptoms and age, and whether to run it is a decision made together with you.
- **Ultrasound.** If the picture calls for it, an [ultrasound](/servicios/ultrasonido) may be ordered to look at the bladder or kidneys, with no needles and no radiation.

## What you can adjust starting today

While you wait for your checkup, these general changes help many men:

- Cut back on coffee and caffeinated sodas, especially after noon.
- Sip water steadily from morning to evening, then ease off once you're about two hours from bedtime.
- Before sleeping, urinate twice a few minutes apart to empty your bladder better.
- Cutting water to make fewer trips backfires, because very concentrated urine bothers the bladder more.
- Don't stop any medicine you were prescribed on your own, even if you suspect it makes you urinate; bring it up at the visit.

## When it can't wait for an appointment

Some situations call for going to an emergency room or calling 911:

- Your bladder feels ready to burst, your lower belly hurts and you can't pass even a few drops.
- Fever spikes and you shiver uncontrollably while every trip to the bathroom stings.
- Your urine is red or has clots and you feel weak or dizzy.
- You get intense, sudden pain in a testicle.
- You feel pressure or pain in your chest, or you're short of breath.
