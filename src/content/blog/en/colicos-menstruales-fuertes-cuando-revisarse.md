In plenty of families the same line gets passed from mother to daughter: "periods hurt, just deal with it." And it's true that some cramping is part of the cycle for most women. But there's a big gap between a dull ache that eases with a heating pad and pain that makes you call in sick, leaves you curled up on the bathroom floor or wakes you at 3 a.m. That second kind of pain isn't something you have to put up with in silence every month.

In this guide, the medical team at Clínica Hispana Cruz 2 explains why cramps happen, how to tell the expected kind from the kind worth checking, and what you can do in the meantime. ## Why periods hurt

During your period, the uterus contracts to shed the lining that builds up inside it. Those contractions are driven by substances called prostaglandins. When the body makes a lot of them, the uterus squeezes harder, blood flow to it dips for moments at a time, and you get a cramp: a dull or spasm-like pain low in the belly that sometimes runs down into the thighs or settles in the lower back.

When no other condition is behind it, this pain is called primary dysmenorrhea. It usually starts in the teen years, shortly after the first periods, and many women notice it eases with age or after giving birth.

There's a second type, secondary dysmenorrhea, where the pain comes from something else: tissue similar to the uterine lining growing outside the uterus (endometriosis), fibroids, a pelvic infection or other changes worth identifying. This kind of cramping tends to show up later in life or changes shape over time.

## Expected cramps or cramps to check

No chart replaces a visit, but this comparison can help you get your bearings:

| Most common | Worth getting checked |
|---|---|
| Starts just before or at the start of your period | Starts several days early or lingers after the bleeding stops |
| Lasts one to three days and fades | Gets longer or stronger every month |
| Eases with heat, rest or an over-the-counter pain reliever | Nothing that used to work helps anymore |
| Lets you get through your day, uncomfortable but upright | Often keeps you home from work or school |
| Has felt the same for years | Showed up or changed suddenly after 25 or 30 |
| Your usual bleeding | Large clots, heavier flow or pain during sex |

If you see yourself mostly in the right-hand column, that doesn't mean something serious is going on. It means it's worth having someone look at it calmly instead of guessing month after month.

## Easing monthly cramps at home

For everyday monthly cramps, these general steps help many women:

- **Heat.** A hot water bottle or heating pad on the lower belly relaxes the muscle of the uterus. Put a towel between it and your skin so you don't burn yourself.
- **Gentle movement.** Walking, stretching or light exercise in the days before and during your period improves blood flow in the pelvis.
- **Over-the-counter pain relievers, used well.** The ones that have worked for you before tend to do more if you start at the first twinge, not once the pain is unbearable. Always follow the label, and ask first if you have stomach problems, kidney problems or take other medicines.
- **Regular sleep and meals.** Being worn out or skipping meals makes any pain feel worse.
- **Keep a log.** Note on your phone the day your period starts, how many days the pain lasts, how bad it is on a 1-to-10 scale and what you did to ease it. That record is gold during a visit.

What to avoid: taking more pills than the package says because "they don't do anything," mixing several pain relievers on your own, or writing off pain that gets worse every month as normal.

## When to come in to the clinic

We suggest a [gynecology](/servicios/ginecologia) visit if:

- Cramps keep you from working, studying or caring for your kids one or more days every month.
- Over-the-counter pain relievers no longer cut it.
- The pain started or changed recently, especially if you're already an adult.
- You also have very heavy periods, bleeding between periods or pain during sex.
- You notice unusual discharge, a bad odor or burning along with the pain.
- You've been trying to get pregnant for a while without success and your periods have always been very painful.

During the visit, the medical team asks about your cycle and how long it has hurt, reviews your symptoms and, if needed, takes a culture to rule out an infection. If the picture calls for it, they may order a pelvic [ultrasound](/servicios/ultrasonido), which Clínica Hispana Cruz 2 does right at the clinic and which shows the uterus and ovaries with no needles and no radiation. For that scan you drink several glasses of water beforehand and arrive with a full bladder, so it's best to call ahead for the instructions.

If your periods are also very heavy and you feel tired all the time, our [blood tests](/servicios/examenes-sangre) include a CBC that shows how your red blood cells are doing. And if there's any chance you could be pregnant, a [pregnancy test](/servicios/prueba-embarazo) comes first, because it completely changes how pain low in the belly is read.

## Birth control can ease cramps too

Many women don't know that, besides preventing pregnancy, hormonal methods often make periods lighter and less painful. At Clínica Hispana Cruz 2 we offer birth control pills and the shot given every three months; at a [birth control](/servicios/anticonceptivos) visit the team checks your blood pressure, your history and what you take to see whether one of them fits you. It isn't the answer for everyone, but it's worth talking through if cramps are your main complaint every month. If a treatment is chosen, the prescription for your cramps or your method travels electronically to the pharmacy you prefer, so there's no paper to carry around.

## Signs that call for the ER or 911

Some pelvic pain isn't a period cramp at all and needs care right away. Go to the emergency room, or call 911 if you can't get there safely, when you notice:

- Very intense pain that comes on suddenly in the lower belly, unlike your usual cramps.
- Sharp pain on one side or vaginal bleeding, with a positive pregnancy test or a late period.
- A high fever together with pelvic pain.
- Bleeding so heavy it soaks through one pad after another, especially if you feel dizzy or pass out.

In those cases, don't wait for the day to end or for your next appointment.
