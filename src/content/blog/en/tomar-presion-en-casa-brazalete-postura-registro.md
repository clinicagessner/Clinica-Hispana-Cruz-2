Monday, 7 a.m. Coffee in one hand, you wrap the cuff over the sleeve of your hoodie, press start and, while it inflates, answer your grandson, who can't find his backpack. The screen reads 152/94. A little alarmed, you move to the dining table, sit quietly for a while, pull the sleeve off and try again: 134/84. Which of the two numbers do you tell the medical team about?

The short answer is that the second one is much closer to the truth, and the first one tells us very little. Blood pressure shifts from minute to minute, and a home monitor only gives you a number you can trust when you use it the same way every time. Here is how to get there, step by step.

## Why home readings matter

The American Heart Association (AHA) [recommends home monitoring](https://www.heart.org/en/health-topics/high-blood-pressure/understanding-blood-pressure-readings/monitoring-your-blood-pressure-at-home) for everyone with high blood pressure, because it shows whether treatment is working. At a visit we only get a snapshot of a few minutes, sometimes with waiting-room nerves on top. A week of your own readings is closer to the whole movie.

It works the other way too: some people look fine at the clinic and run high at home without knowing it. That's why, during your [diabetes and hypertension care](/servicios/condiciones-cronicas) visits, the clinic's medical team looks at the numbers you log at home alongside the ones we take here.

## The device: upper arm, not wrist

The AHA advises an automatic monitor with a cuff that wraps around the upper arm. Wrist and finger models give less reliable readings, since the result changes a lot depending on how you hold your hand. When you buy one, check the box to make sure the model has been validated; if it's for an older adult or for someone who is pregnant, confirm the validation covers them as well.

Cuff size is the step people skip most often. With a sewing tape, measure around your arm halfway between the shoulder and the elbow, then compare that number with the range printed on the cuff. A cuff that is too small squeezes too hard and tends to read high; one that is too big can read low. If your arm is on the larger side, buying the large cuff separately beats forcing the one that came in the box.

## Thirty minutes before, and five minutes before

A good reading starts before the cuff goes on. According to the AHA:

- For the 30 minutes beforehand, skip coffee, caffeinated sodas, cigarettes and exercise
- Use the bathroom first, since a full bladder pushes the number up
- Sit and rest for at least five minutes in silence, with no phone, no TV and no chatting
- If you take blood pressure pills, check in the morning before taking them, unless the medical team told you otherwise

Walking in from work, climbing the stairs to your apartment or arguing on the phone and then measuring right away is the easiest way to get a number that doesn't reflect your everyday pressure.

## Posture, from your feet to your elbow

Run through your body from the ground up before you press the button:

- Feet flat on the floor, legs uncrossed
- Back resting against a chair with a backrest; a deep couch or the edge of the bed won't do
- Arm supported on the table, relaxed, palm facing up
- The middle of the cuff at heart level; if the table sits low, slide a pillow under your arm
- Cuff on bare skin, with its lower edge just above the crease of the elbow
- Snug but not tight: a couple of fingers should slide under it

Rolling up a thick sleeve leaves a band of fabric that squeezes like a tourniquet. Take that layer off or wear short sleeves when you measure.

## Common slip-ups and how to fix them

| What happens | What to do instead |
|---|---|
| Cuff over clothing | Cuff on bare skin |
| Talking while it inflates | Stay quiet until it finishes |
| Arm hanging by your side | Arm resting on the table at heart level |
| Measuring the moment you walk in | Five minutes seated first |
| Small cuff on a large arm | Pick the size that matches your arm measurement |
| Repeating until a nice number shows up | Two readings, and write down both |
| Only checking when you have a headache | A set schedule, whether you feel good or not |

## How many readings, and when

Each time you check, take two readings one minute apart and write down both. Try to do it at the same time every day. A common routine is once in the morning before breakfast and once in the evening before dinner, for the week leading up to your visit. Ask the medical team how often makes sense for you, because someone starting a new treatment needs a different plan than someone who has been stable for years.

Stick to the same arm. The gap between arms is usually small, but switching from day to day muddies the log.

## A log that actually helps at your visit

A notebook by the kitchen, a printed sheet or the monitor's memory all work, as long as you record:

- Date and time
- Both readings: the top number (systolic) and the bottom number (diastolic)
- Your pulse, if the monitor shows it
- Short notes: slept badly, were in pain, missed a pill, had coffee beforehand

Don't round the numbers or erase the ones you don't like; odd readings tell the medical team something too. If your monitor stores readings, bring it to the visit along with the notebook.

The AHA also suggests taking your monitor to an appointment so its reading can be compared with the one in the office and someone can check that you're using it correctly. It's worth repeating that check about once a year, or after the device takes a fall.

## When a number scares you

One high reading on its own is no reason to panic. Sit down, breathe, wait a minute and measure again, following every step. The AHA explains that if your pressure [stays above 180/120](https://www.heart.org/en/health-topics/high-blood-pressure/understanding-blood-pressure-readings/when-to-call-911-for-high-blood-pressure) and you also have chest pain, shortness of breath, back pain, numbness, weakness, vision changes or trouble speaking, you need to call 911. Call right away as well if half of the face sags, an arm loses strength or words come out slurred. Don't drive and don't come to the clinic: wait for the ambulance.

If the number stays that high but you feel fine, contact the medical team promptly. And something we say often: even if your readings come out low several days in a row, don't stop or change your medicines on your own. Any change to your pills gets decided with the medical team first, with your log in front of you.

## When your log shows a pattern

A week of carefully taken readings can say a lot. If your numbers run high consistently, the medical team may adjust your plan and, depending on your situation, order [blood tests](/servicios/examenes-sangre) such as a lipid panel or kidney function. After years of high blood pressure, or when palpitations show up, an [electrocardiogram](/servicios/electrocardiograma) at the same clinic is sometimes added. And if a medicine needs swapping, your [e-prescription](/servicios/farmacia) goes straight to a drugstore you pick, near home or near work.
